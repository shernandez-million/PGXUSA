Home Additions in Hillsboro Beach

Growing a house between two waters.

Land is the scarce thing in Hillsboro Beach. The town holds roughly a third of a square mile of dry ground, strung along one road, and nobody is making more of it. So owners on Hillsboro Mile expand what they have: a rear wing toward the Intracoastal, a second floor over a low 1960s ranch, a garage turned into a guest suite, a conditioned room where a screened porch has been slowly losing its argument with the salt. Estate lots that run from the beach across A1A to a private dock have room in the middle. Most of the work happens there.

Additions here are a zoning exercise before they are a construction one. The Town of Hillsboro Beach reviews site plans at the commission level, and a complete set of drawings is expected two months ahead of the meeting it is calendared for. The code caps most buildings at three stories and thirty-five feet, measured above average dune elevation east of A1A and above the crown of the road on the west side, and holds an 85-foot front setback. Runoff has to stay off A1A, mechanical equipment has to be screened, and oceanward work can fall under the state's coastal construction control line.

Experience on an addition either shows or is conspicuously absent. Tying a new foundation to a fifty-year-old one, matching stucco texture and roof profile so the seam disappears, keeping the occupied half of the house livable and clean while the other half is open to the sky. PGX has delivered more than 150 remodels and additions across South Florida, every one permitted and inspected. We fix the scope in writing, we run the site daily, and we carry the project from the first feasibility conversation through final inspection and warranty without handing you off.

FAQ

Straight answers.

Does my addition have to go before the Town Commission?
If it needs site plan approval, yes. Hillsboro Beach runs site plan review and its Board of Zoning Appeals through the Town Commission itself, and complete, coordinated drawings are expected roughly two months ahead of the meeting. Survey, civil, landscape and architectural sheets all have to agree, with a signed coordination letter from the consultants. It is a small town with a serious process. We build that lead time into the schedule from day one.
How tall can I build on Hillsboro Mile?
Less tall than most people assume. The code holds most of the Mile to three stories and thirty-five feet, measured above average dune elevation on the ocean side of A1A and above the crown of the road on the Intracoastal side, with that dune elevation established on your survey. Decorative features such as cupolas and railings can add a limited amount above the cap. Variances exist, but they are heard by a Board of Zoning Appeals made up of the Town Commission, and the hardship standard is not a formality.
Can I build toward the beach?
Rarely as far as you would like. Construction seaward of Florida's coastal construction control line needs a permit from the state Department of Environmental Protection, the dune and its vegetation are protected, and the town's plans have to show an easterly building line that structures respect. Practically, that pushes most additions toward the road or the Intracoastal side of the property. We map the control line, the dune elevation and the setbacks onto your survey before anyone sketches a room with an ocean view.
How long does an addition take here?
Front-load your patience. Design, structural engineering, site plan review and permitting commonly consume several months before ground breaks, and the town's submittal calendar sets part of that pace. Construction then runs several more months depending on size, elevation work and whether a second story is involved. Twelve months between the first conversation and furniture in the new room is not a pessimistic estimate for a substantial addition; it is the normal one. You get the full timeline in writing, with the review milestones named.
Is adding on worth it, or should I rebuild?
On this island, the arithmetic is unusual. Lots are effectively unrepeatable, and the flood rules mean that once your investment crosses the substantial improvement threshold, the whole structure has to meet current standards anyway. Sometimes that tips the answer toward a full rebuild; often it does not, especially when a house sits at a workable elevation. We run that analysis with real numbers before you commission drawings, and we tell you when the honest answer is not the one that gives us more work.
